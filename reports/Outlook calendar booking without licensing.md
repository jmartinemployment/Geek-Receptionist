# Book Outlook without buying Microsoft licences

A small business can connect its Microsoft 365 or Outlook.com calendar to an AI receptionist for **$0 in additional Microsoft spend**: calendar read and write is a *standard* Microsoft Graph API, "available as part of the user license without additional costs" ([Metered APIs overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/metered-api-overview)), and the only licence involved is the one that already gives the owner a mailbox. The design question is not licensing but **consent** — and on that, the two researchers who looked hardest reached opposite conclusions. The contradiction dissolves: they were describing different client types and different token classes. Microsoft's refresh-token revocation table, read in the **confidential-client** column, says an ordinary user password change and self-service reset leave a server-side refresh token **alive**; the Continuous Access Evaluation page that says "password changed or reset" revokes sessions is written about user sessions and CAE-capable clients, not about a confidential client's refresh token ([Refresh tokens](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/refresh-tokens); [Continuous access evaluation](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity/conditional-access/concept-continuous-access-evaluation)). **Delegated `Calendars.ReadWrite` + `offline_access` on a confidential client is the recommended model.** App-only still loses, though by a narrower margin than this report first claimed: an administrator now has to act either way, so the honest remaining advantages of delegated are that it needs no Exchange Administrator running PowerShell for RBAC-for-Applications scoping, that it exists for personal Outlook.com accounts, and that it never reaches a mailbox whose owner did not sign in. **Admin consent is the normal path, not the error path** — Microsoft's managed consent policy, the default for new tenants, excludes every calendar scope from end-user consent, so the owner clicks "Consent on behalf of your organization" or nobody connects. In a 1–20 person shop the owner is usually the administrator, so this costs a worse consent screen rather than a blocked signup. Graph also hands this product something Google Calendar never has: `transactionId`, a documented client-supplied idempotency key built for the dropped-response case.

## Microsoft charges nothing for calendar; the mailbox licence is the entire bill

Microsoft splits its Graph surface into three billing categories, and calendar CRUD sits in the free one. Standard APIs "perform standard operations (create, read, update, delete) on customer content" and are included with the user's subscription licence; metering applies only to **high-capacity** bulk export endpoints and **advanced** APIs returning "enriched or aggregated data" ([Metered APIs overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/metered-api-overview)). Microsoft goes further and binds itself: moving an included API to metered "would be a non-backward compatible change" subject to its breaking-change policy. Nothing on the `/me/events` reference page names a SKU, add-on or per-call charge ([List events](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/user-list-events)). A free Outlook.com account is covered too — the permissions table carries a **Delegated (personal Microsoft account)** row with the same scopes as work accounts, and the Microsoft APIs Terms of Use name Outlook.com explicitly ([Terms of Use](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/legal/microsoft-apis/terms-of-use)).

The licensing question that actually matters is therefore not "what must they buy for the integration" but "do they have a mailbox at all". Business Basic is the cheapest plan that includes one, at **$7.00/user/month** from 1 July 2026, with Business Standard at **$14.00** ([Microsoft 365 packaging and pricing updates](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/licensing/news/2026-m365-packaging-pricing-updates)). The trap is **Microsoft 365 Apps for business** at $10.00/user/month, which bundles the Outlook *application* but no Exchange mailbox — Microsoft's own no-Teams pricing page leaves the mailbox-storage row blank for that plan ([plans without Teams](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/business/no-teams-plans-and-pricing)). An owner on that plan will still say "we use Outlook"; they mean the desktop app, and their email is at GoDaddy or Google Workspace. Signup must detect this rather than assume it.

Throttling gives enormous headroom and one real constraint. The Outlook service allows **10,000 requests per 10 minutes per app-and-mailbox pair** — roughly 16.7 requests/second against a single calendar, three orders of magnitude above what a 20-person business generates — but only **four concurrent requests** to the same mailbox ([Graph throttling limits](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/throttling-limits)). The binding limit is parallelism, not quota, which is an argument for one `getSchedule` call covering several staff rather than a fan-out of per-person reads. Limits are scoped per mailbox, so one noisy customer cannot throttle another — the property a multi-tenant SaaS needs.

What constrains the product is contractual, not technical. The Microsoft APIs Terms of Use §3(c) permits email and calendar API use only for syncing or backing up events **"unless you have use permissions expressly and specifically granted by Customers"**; §3(b)(4) forbids building databases or copies of API data "except as necessary to enable an intended usage scenario"; §3(b)(5) requires requesting the minimum data and minimum permissions ([Terms of Use](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/legal/microsoft-apis/terms-of-use)). Those three clauses point the same direction as Zero Data Retention already does: query free/busy at call time, mirror nothing, store an event id and nothing more. Two commercial facts belong in any plan that makes Outlook a headline feature — Microsoft's total liability to the developer is capped at **USD $5.00 collectively** (§9(b)) and it may terminate API access "in our sole discretion at any time, for any reason" (§10).

## Consent policy, not licensing, is what can stop a self-serve connection

The scope set is narrow and deliberate: **`offline_access openid profile https://graph.microsoft.com/Calendars.ReadWrite`**. `Calendars.ReadWrite` is a superset of everything the list-calendars call and `getSchedule` need, so no second calendar scope is required, and `offline_access` must appear explicitly in the scope parameter to get a refresh token back ([Scopes and permissions](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/scopes-oidc)). Critically, **do not reach for `findMeetingTimes`**. It forces the `.Shared` family — worded to the owner as "Read and write user **and shared** calendars", a visibly broader ask on the consent screen — is unsupported on personal accounts, has no application permission, and Microsoft warns that its suggestion algorithm "undergoes fine-tuning from time to time" so identical inputs may return different slots ([findMeetingTimes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/user-findmeetingtimes?view=graph-rest-1.0)). A receptionist reading a non-reproducible black box aloud to a caller is the wrong shape. `getSchedule` is the right read: one round trip, up to 20 entities, under 62 days, returning a merged `availabilityView` plus the mailbox's own `workingHours` in the same response ([getSchedule](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/calendar-getschedule?view=graph-rest-1.0)).

Registration must be **`AzureADandPersonalMicrosoftAccount`** if free Outlook.com owners are in scope. That caps the app at 30 permissions instead of 400, which is irrelevant for a 4-scope app, but it costs functionally: a personal account **cannot use `getSchedule` at all**. Availability on consumer accounts must be derived from `calendarView`, which returns event subjects and bodies rather than opaque free/busy — a ZDR problem, not just extra code. Two availability code paths are unavoidable if consumer accounts are supported.

On the self-serve question, the permission's own flag says yes: `Calendars.ReadWrite` is delegated with **"Admin consent required: No"** ([Permissions reference](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/permissions-reference)), and "by default, all users are allowed to consent to applications for permissions that don't require administrator consent" ([Configure how users consent](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/enterprise-apps/configure-user-consent)). But the flag is half the answer. **Risk-based step-up consent is enabled by default** and blocks user consent to "most newly registered multitenant apps that aren't publisher verified" — specifically apps registered after 8 November 2020 requesting permissions beyond basic sign-in from a foreign tenant ([Publisher verification overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/publisher-verification-overview)). That is this product exactly. Without verification the owner hits **`AADSTS90094`** and must find an admin ([Configure risk-based step-up consent](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/enterprise-apps/configure-risk-based-step-up-consent)).

Publisher verification therefore still matters, but for trust rather than as the gate this report first made it: risk-based step-up consent blocks *user* consent, and user consent to a calendar scope is already unavailable on a managed-policy tenant. What verification buys is a consent screen that names a verified publisher instead of warning about an unverified one, and the legacy-policy tenants where self-consent is still possible. Start the enrolment early anyway — the long pole is the Partner Program, not the verification. It costs nothing — "Microsoft doesn't charge developers for publisher verification. No license is required" — and takes minutes *for developers who already meet the prerequisites*. The prerequisites are the work: a **Partner One ID** for a verified Microsoft AI Cloud Partner Program partner *global* account (not a location ID), the app registered from a work or school account (**apps registered using a Microsoft account can't be publisher verified**), a publisher domain that isn't `*.onmicrosoft.com`, and MFA sign-in by someone holding both Application Administrator in Entra and Partner Admin in Partner Center. The application object also **cannot be moved between tenants** once created, so the registration must be made in the tenant the vendor intends to keep.

**Verification does not buy self-consent, though, and that was checked after this report's first draft.** Microsoft's [Manage app consent policies](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/enterprise-apps/manage-app-consent-policies) page says the Microsoft-managed policy "is also the default for a new tenant," and that under it "End users can consent for any user consentable delegated permissions EXCEPT" a named list — which includes `Calendars.Read`, `Calendars.ReadBasic`, `Calendars.ReadWrite`, `Calendars.Read.Shared` and `Calendars.ReadWrite.Shared`. The exclusion is keyed to the permission, not to the publisher's verification status, so a verified publisher asking for `Calendars.ReadWrite` is refused exactly as an unverified one is, and there is no narrower calendar scope to retreat to. **On a default-policy tenant the owner cannot self-consent at all; an administrator must.** A tenant moved to `microsoft-user-default-low` blocks it for a second, independent reason — consent only to verified publishers and only for permissions that tenant's admin has classified "low impact," which `Calendars.ReadWrite` will not be. The honest statement is now that **admin consent is the main path and self-consent is the lucky case, on a tenant still running the legacy policy** — and the product cannot see which it is before trying. Build the admin-approval path as the primary flow, detect `AADSTS90094`, and say so plainly rather than failing blank. This is survivable because the target is a 1–20 person business whose owner generally *is* the Global Administrator, so "Consent on behalf of your organization" is a checkbox they can tick themselves; and because admin consent to a *delegated* permission pre-approves the permission without granting tenant-wide reach — the app still acts only as whoever signed in. Say that in the onboarding copy, because the owner will otherwise assume the opposite. Detail and dates: `research_notes/Outlook calendar booking without licensing/default-consent-policy-and-calendar-scopes.md`. The competitor research corroborates that this is the shipped norm: IONOS, Allo, Nextiva and Voksha all document a plain "Sign in with Microsoft" + consent dialog clicked by the owner with no admin mentioned, and only My AI Front Desk documents the conditional — "admin consent if your tenant restricts third-party apps" ([My AI Front Desk](https://llms.myaifrontdesk.com/outlook-microsoft-bookings-integration)).

## The delegated-versus-app-only contradiction dissolves once you separate client types

Two notes reached opposite conclusions, and the resolution decides the whole design.

**What `graph-oauth-and-consent.md` found.** Microsoft publishes a revocation table broken out by client type. Reading the **confidential client** column: password expires → *stays alive*; password changed by user → *stays alive*; user performs self-service password reset → *stays alive*; admin resets password from the Azure portal → *stays alive*; admin resets password from the **Entra admin center** → *revoked*; admin resets from the **M365 admin center** → *revoked*; user or admin explicitly revokes refresh tokens → *revoked* ([Refresh tokens](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/refresh-tokens)). Default lifetime is **90 days**, rolling — except **24 hours** if the redirect URI is registered as `spa`, with that short expiry carrying over to every subsequent token.

**What `booking-mechanics-and-pitfalls.md` found.** Continuous Access Evaluation treats "Password for a user is changed or reset" as a critical event, CAE revokes user sessions and refresh tokens on that event, and "critical event evaluation doesn't rely on Conditional Access policies so it's available in any tenant" ([Continuous access evaluation](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity/conditional-access/concept-continuous-access-evaluation)). From that it concluded delegated tokens would die mid-call and app-only was forced.

**They are not describing the same thing, in three distinct ways.** First, *client class*: the revocation table exists precisely because Entra treats confidential clients — a server-side app authenticating with its own secret — differently from public clients; the CAE page makes no such split and is written about user sessions. Second, *token class*: CAE's mechanism is a resource re-evaluating a long-lived access token (up to 28 hours for CAE-aware clients) against a claims challenge, while the revocation table governs the refresh token itself. Third, *actor*: the table distinguishes "changed by the user", "self-service reset" and "admin reset", and even distinguishes *which admin portal performed it* — a granularity flatly incompatible with a blanket reading of "changed or reset". The refresh-tokens page is the specific authority on the exact object in question. **The second note's conclusion is a generalisation of a sentence not written about a confidential client's refresh token, and it does not hold.**

That said, the first note's table does not make delegated risk-free, and the report should not oversell it. An **admin-initiated reset from the Entra or M365 admin center does revoke** — and in a 1–20 person business the owner usually *is* the Global Administrator, and the M365 admin center is exactly where they would reset a staff password. So the accurate statement is: the grant survives ordinary password changes and SSPR, and dies on an admin-portal reset, on explicit revocation, or on 90 days of inactivity.

Even granting the second note's premise, **app-only would not rescue it**, because app-only fails earlier and harder. Only a Privileged Role Administrator or Global Administrator can consent to application permissions ([Permissions overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/permissions-overview)). Scoping that grant to one mailbox now means Exchange **RBAC for Applications**, which "replaces Application Access Policies" and requires an Exchange Administrator running `New-ServicePrincipal`, `New-ManagementScope` and `New-ManagementRoleAssignment` in PowerShell — and then *remembering to remove the Entra-level grant*, because "the union of an unscoped grant from Microsoft Entra and a resource-scoped grant in Application RBAC results in **no effective resource scoping**" ([RBAC for Applications](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/exchange/permissions-exo/application-rbac)). Permission changes there cache for 30 minutes to 2 hours. Unscoped, the app holds read-write access to *every mailbox in the tenant*, which violates Terms §3(b)(5) on minimum permissions. And **application permissions do not exist for personal Microsoft accounts at all**, so the consumer path vanishes. Finally, app-only breaks this estate's stated architecture: the credential becomes a vendor secret rather than something the owner authorises by signing into their own account, so there is no "owner signs in at the vendor's own endpoint" step for GeekAPI's OAuth to hold.

The "mid-call failure" framing is itself the error. A revoked grant surfaces as `invalid_grant` **at refresh time, not mid-sentence** — provided refresh happens out of band on a schedule rather than lazily when a caller is already on the line. The fail-loud design follows: refresh proactively, treat `invalid_grant` as an expected recoverable state, mark the tenant's booking capability disabled and surface "reconnect your calendar" to the owner, and never let the voice agent offer a slot it cannot write. That is containment that reaches an operator, which this estate's rules already require.

**Recommendation: delegated `Calendars.ReadWrite` + `offline_access`, confidential client, server-side, redirect URI registered as `web` and never `spa`.**

## Graph gives a double-booking guarantee that Google does not

`event.transactionId` is **"a custom identifier specified by a client app for the server to avoid redundant POST operations in case of client retries to create the same event... useful when low network connectivity causes the client to time out before receiving a response"** ([event resource type](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/resources/event?view=graph-rest-1.0)). It cannot be changed after creation and is readable back, so it doubles as the reconciliation key. The live-call sequence is: persist the transaction id *before* the POST, retry the identical POST once on timeout, and if still uncertain read back by `transactionId` via `calendarView` before telling the caller anything. Never create a second event; never confirm an unverified write.

Four mechanics decide whether this works on real tenants. **Invitations cannot be suppressed** — when an event has attendees "the server sends invitations to all attendees... and can't be configured", and Graph has no equivalent of Google's `sendUpdates=none`. The right model is an appointment with `attendees: []`, the caller's details in subject and body, `showAs: "busy"`, and confirmation sent through the product's own channel. That is simultaneously the ZDR-correct answer. **`availabilityView` conflates free with working-elsewhere** — `0` means free *or* workingElsewhere, so an owner marked working-elsewhere reads as bookable; read `scheduleItems[].status` instead. **`workingHours` is not a source of truth** — it is optional configuration the owner may never have touched, with a documented default of Mon–Fri 08:00–17:00 that is wrong for a salon, a Saturday clinic or a trade starting at 7am, and its `timeZone` comes back in two shapes (a Windows name or a `#microsoft.graph.customTimeZone` object with bias and offsets) that will throw parse-time on real mailboxes. Opening hours belong in the tenant's own configuration, collected in the signup form. **Hybrid mailboxes cannot be made to work** — community reports show `MailboxNotEnabledForRESTAPI` on on-premises mailboxes, and Microsoft acknowledged a 2026 defect where delegated on-premises mailboxes cannot see Exchange Online free/busy in Graph-only hybrid ([support 5127092](https://support.microsoft.com/servicing/exchange/server/update/2026/5127092)).

The conclusion for onboarding is firm: **consent succeeding proves nothing**. Make a real `getSchedule` call against the real target mailbox at signup, check `mailboxSettings.userPurpose` for `shared`/`room`/`equipment`, and refuse onboarding loudly on failure rather than deferring the discovery to a caller.

## Agent 365 asks about $37 per user per month to reach the same Graph calls

Agent 365 went GA on **1 May 2026** and is a per-user-licensed governance plane for AI agents, at **$15/user/month standalone** or bundled in Microsoft 365 E7 at $99 ([Microsoft Licensing FAQ 122](https://www.microsoft.com/licensing/faqs/122); [Microsoft Security Blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/05/01/microsoft-agent-365-now-generally-available-expands-capabilities-and-integrations)). Its SMB baseline is **Business Premium** — $22/user/month — plus the Defender and Purview suites for SMB "to unlock Agent 365 functionality". Stacking the two published figures puts a Business Standard shop at roughly **$37/user/month before the suites**, against $14 today.

The calendar MCP server itself, `mcp_CalendarTools` at `https://agent365.svc.cloud.microsoft/agents/tenants/{tenantId}/servers/mcp_CalendarTools`, is preview and carries a deprecation-in-place notice telling you not to use it: application-specific M365 MCP tools are **"only intended for backward compatibility purposes and shouldn't be used in production environments or new projects"** ([Calendar reference (preview)](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-agent-365/mcp-server-reference/calendar)). Its tools are named `mcp_CalendarTools_graph_findMeetingTimes`, `_graph_getSchedule`, `_graph_createEvent` — literal wrappers over the same Graph calls. **The MCP route buys governance and Copilot discoverability, not capability.** The successor it points to, Work IQ MCP, is worse for this buyer: **"You pay for use of the Work IQ API through a usage-based model that uses Copilot Credits"**, requiring a Copilot Studio billing plan against an Azure subscription and a **Global Administrator** for one-time setup ([Work IQ API overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/extensibility/work-iq-api-overview); [Enable Work IQ](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/extensibility/work-iq/enable-work-iq)). No first-party Microsoft calendar MCP surface was found that sits outside Agent 365 or Work IQ.

The pattern is deliberate and worth naming: **Microsoft meters the agentic reasoning layer and leaves the data plane included with the user's licence.** A product that does its own reasoning and calls Graph for data pays nothing extra. Geek-Receptionist is on the right side of that line, and the $37 contrast is the sentence to say to a customer's IT person — a story to tell, not a route to build.

Microsoft Bookings deserves a separate verdict because it is genuinely tempting and genuinely wrong here. `getStaffAvailability` returns *bookable* availability rather than raw free/busy, modelling services, duration and staff natively, and it is **app-only** (delegated "Not supported") ([getStaffAvailability](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/bookingbusiness-getstaffavailability?view=graph-rest-1.0)). But Microsoft's own getting-started sequence opens with "Make sure the business has a **Microsoft 365 Business Premium** subscription" ([Bookings API overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/resources/booking-api-overview?view=graph-rest-1.0)), it stores `bookingCustomer` records and sends its own notification emails from the tenant — a new place caller details persist and a new outbound message whose wording the product does not control — and **no idempotency key equivalent to `transactionId` is documented on `bookingAppointment`**. Richer product fit, weaker engineering fit, and a direct collision with ZDR.

## Seven of eight competitors have no Outlook story at all

The most useful competitive finding is how little competition there is. Of eight named AI receptionist vendors, **not one was verified to do direct, native, read-and-write Microsoft 365 booking through its own Graph OAuth connection**. Rosie lists exactly four scheduling integrations — Calendly, Google Calendar, Appointlet, Acuity — with **Microsoft absent from both the current list and "Coming Soon"** ([Rosie, Scheduling Integrations](https://heyrosie.com/support/en/articles/12998658-scheduling-integrations)). Dialpad's Microsoft 365 integration is free on every plan and is **read-only** — presence and contact profiles, no event creation ([Dialpad, Microsoft 365](https://ecosystem.dialpad.com/partners/microsoft-365/)). Smith.ai's route is a Bookings link the owner emails them — "just send us each individual's Bookings link" ([Smith.ai](https://smith.ai/integrates-with/microsoft-office-365)) — and its AI Receptionist product documents only Calendly. Slang.ai books into OpenTable; Numa into dealership systems.

ElevenLabs is the one vendor that states a reason, and it does not survive scrutiny. Its docs say Microsoft Graph **"only accepts OAuth sign-ins"**, so Reception connects through Zapier at **two Zapier tasks per successful call** ([ElevenLabs, Microsoft Outlook](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/reception-ai/integrations/microsoft-outlook)). Nextiva's published setup disproves it: a direct Graph integration with an account-type picker (Common / Tenant-specific / Consumer / Organization), authorization-code grant by default with client-credentials offered for tenant-specific unattended use, and named scopes `Calendars.Read`, `Calendars.ReadWrite`, `Calendars.Read.Shared`, `Calendars.ReadWrite.Shared` plus always-requested `User.Read` and `offline_access` ([Nextiva, Outlook Calendar Setup](https://help.nextiva.com/article/nextiva-next-outlook-calendar-setup-guide)). ElevenLabs' sentence means "we chose not to build an Entra app registration, consent flow and per-tenant token store" — the same build-versus-buy decision this product faces, resolved the other way.

Two conclusions follow. The vendors that *do* ship first-party Outlook — Nextiva, IONOS, Allo, Voksha — all use **plain delegated OAuth with a self-serve Microsoft consent dialog**, exactly the model recommended here. And the market pattern is not "Outlook costs more" but **"Outlook doesn't exist"**: where tiering exists it gates calendar integration as a category with Google and Microsoft treated identically, and IONOS states this explicitly ([IONOS AI Frontdesk](https://www.ionos.com/help/ai/ai-frontdesk/appointment-scheduling-and-integrations/ai-frontdesk-setting-up-appointment-booking-with-google-calendar-or-microsoft-365/)). Shipping native Outlook booking is a differentiator against Rosie, whose pricing this product is positioned a dollar under.

### Third-party unified calendar APIs: publisher verification and ZDR split the field, not price

The fifth researcher's notes arrived in time and this section rests on them, not on inference from absence. One finding carries forward from the competitor research regardless: **no AI receptionist vendor was found using Nylas, Cronofy, Merge, Paragon or Composio for calendars** — these vendors sell to CRM, ATS and scheduling products.

**Who carries the Microsoft registration burden is the sharpest dividing line.** Nylas structurally cannot remove it: its Microsoft connector is configured with *your* Azure app's client ID and secret, and its own guide puts all four publisher-verification steps on the developer including joining the Microsoft AI Cloud Partner Program ([Nylas: Creating an Azure auth app](https://developer.nylas.com/docs/provider-guides/microsoft/create-azure-app/); [Nylas verification guide](https://developer.nylas.com/docs/provider-guides/microsoft/verification-guide/)). Cronofy uses its own application, "Cronofy for Office 365" (`f573e3d3-ea9c-4d8d-9d8f-20107b8cedbd`), and supplies a **pre-generated admin-consent URL** in its portal — manually constructing one is unsupported and causes a state error ([Cronofy: 'Need admin approval'](https://docs.cronofy.com/calendar-admins/faqs/need-admin-approval-error/)). coss.com (Cal.com, Inc.) uses its own apps by default with the option to swap yours in later, non-retroactively ([coss.com OAuth apps](https://coss.com/docs/scheduling/oauth-apps)). **Whether Cronofy's and coss.com's Microsoft apps carry the verified-publisher badge is not determined by either vendor's documentation** — and that single fact decides whether an owner in a step-up-consent tenant can self-consent. It is a direct question to put to both.

**Zero Data Retention separates them harder than price does.** Cronofy's own Data Management document lists stored event fields as summaries, descriptions, start/end, recurrence, categories, colours, conferencing URLs, location including latitude and longitude, and **attendee email, name and status** — with no documented off switch ([Cronofy Data Management](https://docs.cronofy.com/policies/data-management/)). If a tenant's calendar contains "Consult — Jane Doe, oncology", that string sits in Cronofy's store; short retention is not zero retention. coss.com is the opposite by construction: **"Stored: when a calendar is busy. Nothing else"**, titles, attendees and descriptions not read or stored, bookings written as a private "Appointment" with no attendees, **a calendar that can't be read treated as busy rather than free**, and overlapping bookings for the same host rejected at the database including from retries and simultaneous requests ([coss.com calendars](https://coss.com/docs/scheduling/calendars); [coss.com scheduling](https://coss.com/scheduling)). Those are the two properties most often got wrong when hand-built, enforced at the read rather than asserted in a policy. Nylas has a passthrough mode that stores nothing, but its documentation frames passthrough-versus-synced around *messages* and IMAP; **whether Google and Microsoft calendar reads are passthrough by default is not determined, and that unknown alone decides whether Nylas is viable here**.

Price then eliminates most of what is left. coss.com is **$0 free, $25/month as credit against usage, $0.10 per confirmed booking** with holds and reschedules unbilled; Nylas Essentials is **$15/month**; Cronofy's entry plan is **$819/month billed annually** — about **$9,828 before the first customer connects a calendar** ([coss.com pricing](https://coss.com/pricing); [Nylas pricing](https://www.nylas.com/pricing/); [Cronofy OEM pricing](https://www.cronofy.com/api/markdown/api-pricing)). Merge.dev has **no calendar category at all** ([Merge pricing](https://www.merge.dev/pricing)), and Paragon, Composio and Zapier are connection plumbing — even where a connector exists, availability computation, slot holds, conflict rejection, recurrence and DST remain yours. One caveat the recommendation must inherit: the researcher's conclusion that **coss.com is the successor to Cal.com Platform rests on a footer and a redirect, not on a statement by either company**, and should be confirmed with sales before anything is built on it.

**Recommendation: build delegated Graph OAuth directly.** It is the only route that satisfies all four stated product constraints simultaneously. The owner connects self-serve at Microsoft's own endpoint with their own account, which is exactly the integration shape this estate specifies; GeekAPI holds the refresh token and geek-mcp-gateway stays stateless, receiving an access token per call; nothing mirrors calendar content, so ZDR holds by construction rather than by vendor policy; and `getSchedule` plus a `transactionId`-keyed `POST /events` is two round trips to one service inside a conversational pause, with a documented answer to the double-booking question. It costs the customer nothing and it ships a feature Rosie does not have at any tier. **Start the Microsoft AI Cloud Partner Program enrolment now** — it is the long-lead item, it is free in money, and without the verified-publisher badge the self-serve flow hits `AADSTS90094` on most tenants.

**Keep coss.com as the named fallback**, to be invoked if publisher verification stalls or if the per-tenant token store proves more operational burden than the $25/month floor plus $0.10 per booking. Its fail-closed-on-unreadable-calendar and database-level overlap rejection are the two correctness properties worth buying. The cost is that a third party sits between GeekAPI and the calendar, the consent screen says coss.com rather than Geek @ Your Spot, and the whole Cal.com-successor thesis is unconfirmed.

### What is unverified and must be tested against a real tenant

Seven things cannot be settled by reading and should be run against a throwaway Microsoft 365 trial tenant before the booking path is written. **First**, whether a CAE critical event — specifically an admin-initiated password reset — revokes a *confidential-client* refresh token in a tenant with no Conditional Access policies. The two Microsoft pages can be read as conflicting and only a test settles which governs. **Second**, the default user-consent policy for a tenant created in 2026: a 2022 Entra blog says new tenants follow "the latest Microsoft recommendation" and end users can no longer consent to multi-tenant apps ([Entra change announcements, June 2022](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoft-entra-blog/microsoft-entra-change-announcements---june-2022-train/2967455)), while current Learn pages describe an unrestricted default — read `GET /v1.0/policies/authorizationPolicy` and inspect `defaultUserRolePermissions.permissionGrantPoliciesAssigned`. **Third** — now settled, and it did change onboarding from an error path to a first-class step; the remaining question is narrower. The Zeeg claim checked out against Microsoft's own [Manage app consent policies](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/enterprise-apps/manage-app-consent-policies) page: every calendar scope is excluded from end-user consent under the Microsoft-managed policy, which is the default for new tenants (Graph permissions from end of October 2025, EWS/EAS/IMAP/POP from end of November 2025, per MC1163922). What is still unknown is **how many existing small-business tenants were migrated onto that policy** versus left on `microsoft-user-default-legacy`, where the exclusion does not apply — which decides whether self-consent is a rare lucky case or a common one. **Fourth**, `transactionId`'s undocumented semantics — maximum length, dedupe scope (per calendar or per mailbox), how long the server remembers it, and what status code a duplicate returns. **Fifth**, end-to-end `getSchedule` and `POST /events` latency from the gateway's region; Microsoft publishes no SLO and "within seconds on a live call" cannot be asserted from documentation. **Sixth**, whether an all-day event left as `showAs: free` fails to block a slot, which is a plausible real-world double-booking cause. **Seventh**, whether `Calendars.ReadWrite` without the `.Shared` variant suffices to call `getSchedule` against another mailbox in the same tenant — which decides whether multi-staff businesses are supportable on the narrow scope.

## Conclusion

The question "how do we add Outlook without the business buying extra licensing" turns out to have a trivial licensing answer and a difficult consent answer, and most of the industry has mistaken the second for the first. Nobody avoids Outlook because Microsoft charges for the calendar — Microsoft does not. They avoid it because Entra app registration, publisher verification, consent policies, work-versus-personal account types and Exchange quirks are a week of unglamorous work that Google Calendar does not demand, so Google ships and Microsoft slips. That is why Rosie has no Microsoft story at any tier and ElevenLabs routes through Zapier behind a sentence about Graph that is not true. The moat here is small and entirely procedural, which is the best kind to cross early.

The sharper insight is that the credential argument was never really about security. Both notes were trying to answer "what dies, and when does the owner find out", and app-only looked safer only because the failure it avoids is loud while the failures it introduces — a tenant-wide grant that nobody scoped, a cached RBAC change, an `ApplicationAccessPolicy` an admin set last quarter — are quiet. Delegated is the right answer partly because its failure mode is honest: a grant that dies produces `invalid_grant` at refresh, on a schedule the product controls, far from any caller. Build the reconnect path as a first-class state rather than an error, prove mailbox access with a real `getSchedule` at signup instead of trusting that consent succeeded, and the remaining risk is a list of seven things that one trial tenant and an afternoon will settle.
